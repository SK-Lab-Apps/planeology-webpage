## /learn/what-is-a-stall

| Claim | Source | Status |
|---|---|---|
| A given airplane always stalls at the same angle of attack regardless of airspeed, weight, load factor or density altitude; the stall speed is not a fixed value | PHAK, PDF p.122 | ✅ |
| Three situations in which the critical angle is most often exceeded: low speed, high speed and turning; example of an airplane in a 100-knot dive pulled up sharply, stalling well above its normal stall speed | PHAK, PDF p.122 | ✅ |
| Stall speed is higher in a level turn (weight plus the turning load); stall speed increases in proportion to the square root of the load factor | PHAK, PDF p.122 and p.131 (Load Factors and Stalling Speeds) | ✅ |
| Near the stall, total drag rises | PHAK, PDF p.105 | ✅ |
| A rectangular wing tends to stall first at the root, giving adequate stall warning and aileron effectiveness; an elliptical wing gives little advance warning; other planforms are tailored with wing twist | PHAK, PDF p.119 | ✅ |
| Spin: "an aggravated stall that results in an airplane descending in a helical, or corkscrew path" | PHAK, PDF p.504 (glossary) | ✅ |
| Stick pusher: at approximately one knot above stall speed it moves the stick forward automatically; T-tail aircraft characteristically pitch up when stalled nose-high and the pusher inhibits that stall | PHAK, PDF p.146 | ✅ |
| Deep stall: at very high angle of attack, low airspeed and aft CG the wing's wake blankets the horizontal tail and elevator control is reduced or eliminated; can occur on any aircraft but more likely with T-tails and tail-mounted engines, so more prevalent on transport than general aviation aircraft | PHAK, PDF p.154 | ✅ |
| Compressor stall: compressor blades are small airfoils; a compressor stall occurs when the blades' angle of attack exceeds the critical angle; air in the compressor slows, stagnates and sometimes reverses | PHAK, PDF p.183 | ✅ |
| The FAA (with the GAJSC) promotes AOA indicators; loss of control is the number one root cause of fatalities in general aviation and commercial aviation; stall/spin scenarios figure in many maneuvering accidents | PHAK, PDF p.123 | ✅ Written as "stalls figure in many of those" rather than quoting the 25-percent/half figures |
| Lift varies almost linearly with angle of attack within about ±10 degrees; the boundary layer separates at high angles | NASA Glenn, "Effects of Inclination on Lift" (app source; the ±10° range is in the app's fact log for `airfoils-aoa`) | ✅ |
| The Boeing 727 has a T-shaped tail and three rear-mounted engines | Boeing, "Select Products in Boeing History", 727 section | ✅ |
| Everything else (a stall is not an engine problem; critical angle 16–20°; any speed, any attitude; a stalled wing still makes some lift; stall warning systems and the stick shaker's 5–7 percent; chord line, camber and angle of attack; symmetrical airfoils and inverted flight; the Pitts spin and the Extra 300; the Wright brothers fun fact) | App lessons `why-planes-stall` and `airfoils-aoa` and their Hangar cards | ✅ |
