## /learn/what-are-contrails

| Claim | Source | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Combustion products in airplane engine exhaust: 71% carbon dioxide, 28% water vapor and less than 1% other products (sulfur oxides, nitrogen oxides, carbon monoxide, unburned hydrocarbons, soot) | FAA, "Contrails" (last updated July 21, 2025), https://www.faa.gov/contrails; FAA, "Contrails Fact Sheet", https://www.faa.gov/contrails/fact-sheet, p.3 | ✅ Written as "what comes out of the combustion", since the exhaust also carries the air that passes through the engine |
| Air at contrail levels can be as cold as −70 °F, so only a little humidity is needed for condensation | FAA, "Contrails Fact Sheet", p.2 | ✅ |
| Exhaust vapor condenses onto fine particles, from the engine or already in the atmosphere, and the droplets quickly freeze; the FAA compares contrails with seeing your breath on a cold day | FAA, "Contrails Fact Sheet", p.2–3; FAA, "Contrails" | ✅ |
| NASA's chart: high-altitude air is generally cold and dry, exhaust is hot and moist; as they mix, a contrail forms only if the mixing line crosses the condensation curve | NASA Langley, "Contrail Identification Chart and Formation Guide", https://scool.larc.nasa.gov/pdf/Contrail_ID_Chart_English_2013_v18_copy.pdf (panels 1–4) | ✅ |
| Contrails are mostly ice crystals rather than streams of pollutants | NASA Earthdata, "On the Trail of Contrails" (December 28, 2020), https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/news/feature-articles/trail-contrails | ✅ |
| Contrails are made primarily of water, as ice crystals, and pose no direct health risk to humans | FAA, "Contrails Fact Sheet", p.1 | ✅ |
| Fine particles occur throughout the atmosphere, so only the exhaust's water vapor is required; in humid air the new crystals keep growing by taking water from the surrounding air | FAA, "Contrails Fact Sheet", p.2–3 | ✅ |
| Sulfur oxides and unburned hydrocarbons in the exhaust can slow the melting of the ice crystals and help contrails persist | FAA, "Contrails Fact Sheet", p.3 | ✅ |
| Contrails won't form if the air at the airplane's altitude is too warm or too dry; humidity at the ground can differ greatly from humidity aloft | FAA, "Contrails Fact Sheet", p.2; FAA, "Contrails" | ✅ |
| Most commercial jets cruise at 26,000 feet or higher, cold enough to freeze the vapor at once; lower-altitude aircraft don't make contrails because the air isn't cold enough | NASA Earthdata, "On the Trail of Contrails" | ✅ (the 26,000-feet figure is also in the app's `why-jets-fly-high` lesson) |
| Small airplanes (Cessna) typically fly at about 2,500 to 9,500 feet | NASA, "Where in the Air Classroom Activity Educator Guide", https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/where-in-the-air-classroom-activity-educator-guide.pdf | ✅ Verified earlier today for /learn/why-do-planes-fly-so-high; not re-fetched |
| Trails from low-flying planes at air shows are special effects made with smoke | NASA Earthdata, "On the Trail of Contrails" | ✅ The Pitts Special is named only as an example of an aerobatic air-show airplane (app Hangar card `pitts-special`) |
| A contrail can appear to start or stop abruptly as the airplane flies in and out of ice supersaturation regions, pockets of humid air; the exhaust has not changed | FAA, "Contrails Fact Sheet", p.3; FAA, "Contrails" | ✅ |
| Three types: short-lived (dry air; the ice sublimates; length stays about constant; gone within seconds to minutes); persistent (very humid air; a few minutes to more than a day; stays a thin line for hours where wind shear is low); persistent spreading (very humid air; hours to days; spread across the sky by high wind shear; can resemble natural cirrus) | NASA Langley chart (panels 2, 5–7); FAA, "Contrails Fact Sheet", p.2 | ✅ |
| Finger test: a persistent contrail is thinner than one finger held at arm's length, a spreading one is wider | NASA Langley chart (panels 6–7) | ✅ |
| Spread contrails are hard to tell apart from natural cirrus, and natural cirrus can be mistaken for contrails | FAA, "Contrails Fact Sheet", p.3 | ✅ |
| In the satellite images the NASA team used, a contrail shows up only once it is about 1 km wide, the size of the image pixels | NASA Earthdata (NASA scientist Douglas Spangenberg on MODIS data) | ✅ |
| Did you know? Persistent contrails drift with upper winds that can reach hundreds of miles per hour, so a contrail seen from the ground may have been made by an airplane hundreds of miles away that has already landed | FAA, "Contrails" | ✅ |
| Clouds reflect sunlight (cooling) and trap heat (warming); a satellite study of linear contrails using 2006 Aqua MODIS data found a net warming effect, "acting like a light blanket"; globally small (the contrail effect would have to grow roughly 100 times to equal all human-made carbon dioxide); the greatest regional warming was along the North Atlantic flight corridors, with central Europe another hot spot; only linear contrails were counted, so the team suspected "at least a factor of three" more | NASA Earthdata, "On the Trail of Contrails" (the study: Bedka et al., Duda et al. and Spangenberg et al., Geophysical Research Letters, 2013) | ✅ |
| Cloud cover in certain flight corridors is estimated to have increased by as much as 20 percent | FAA, "Contrails Fact Sheet", p.4 (citing NWS Flagstaff, "Clouds and Contrails") | ✅ |
| Persistent contrails are a human-caused increase in Earth's cloudiness and are likely to be affecting climate | NASA Langley chart | ✅ |
| Research areas include forecasting the humid regions where persistent contrails form, re-routing around ice supersaturation regions, engine technology and alternative fuels | FAA, "Contrails"; FAA, "Contrails Fact Sheet", p.4 | ✅ |
| Persistent contrails do not indicate that an airplane dispersed harmful chemicals; the US government is not conducting weather modification or releasing harmful agents; jet fuel contains no metal-based compounds, because dissolved metals would affect engine stability | FAA, "Contrails Fact Sheet", p.4 | ✅ |
| Exhaust pollutants at ground level can pose health concerns, and the EPA, FAA and other agencies collaborate on research and standards | FAA, "Contrails"; FAA, "Contrails Fact Sheet", p.3 | ✅ |
| Contrails can change color with the sun (white, gray, red, orange, yellow) because sunlight reflects off their ice crystals | FAA, "Contrails" | ✅ |
| In very humid conditions some aircraft trail contrails that spiral out behind each wingtip | NASA Earthdata | ✅ |
| The Boeing 747 has four engines | Boeing, "Select Products in Boeing History" (747 specifications) | ✅ "Each engine can draw its own trail before they merge" is written as an observation, as the lesson art shows |
| Jet streams can reach 250 mph or more | NOAA NESDIS, "What Is the Jet Stream?" | ✅ app fact (`turbulence-jetstreams-contrails`) |
| Everything else (contrails are clouds made by airplanes; exhaust water vapor condenses around tiny particles and freezes into ice in the cold air; short-lived in dry air, persistent in colder, moister air, and some spread into sheets like cirrus; temperature falls about 2 °C per 1,000 feet in the lower atmosphere; wind shear as a sudden change in wind speed or direction over a short distance; jet streams; the Pitts Special as an aerobatic biplane) | App lessons `turbulence-jetstreams-contrails` and `why-jets-fly-high`, and the Hangar card `pitts-special` | ✅ |

The FAA fact sheet cited is the current one at faa.gov/contrails/fact-sheet (a 4-page PDF from the EPA, FAA and NOAA, served with a last-modified date of July 21, 2025). An older 2000 EPA fact sheet with different figures exists; it is not used.
