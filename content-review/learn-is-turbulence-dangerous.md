## /learn/is-turbulence-dangerous

| Claim | Source | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Clear air turbulence (CAT) is high-altitude turbulence met outside convective cloud, usually tied to wind shear; found near jet streams, the tropopause and upper-air fronts; most often on the poleward side of the jet; stronger above and downwind of mountain ranges | FAA, Advisory Circular 00-30C, *Clear Air Turbulence Avoidance* (March 22, 2016), §5.1, §6.1, §6.4, §6.7.3, https://www.faa.gov/documentLibrary/media/Advisory_Circular/AC_00-30C.pdf | ✅ |
| It is the change in wind speed (shear), not the speed itself, that causes CAT; a jet core of about 110 knots is the usual threshold; the probability rises with how fast the wind drops away from the core | FAA AC 00-30C §6.7.1 | ✅ |
| A typical CAT area is 100 to 300 miles long, 50 to 100 miles wide and about 5,000 feet deep, persisting from 30 minutes to a day (Did you know?) | FAA AC 00-30C §6.7 | ✅ |
| Since the 1990s airliners automatically report wind and temperature (AMDAR); in 2014 NOAA received about 140,000 observations a day from more than 4,000 aircraft; airlines add turbulence as eddy dissipation rate (EDR), an ICAO standard measure of the air rather than of one aircraft type; automated forecasts (GTG) cover the US and the world; dispatchers relay pilot reports | FAA AC 00-30C §7.1, §7.1.1, §7.1.2, §7.1.3, footnote 1 | ✅ |
| Crews use a designated turbulence-penetration (rough-air) speed; reducing speed in rough air lowers gust loads | FAA AC 00-30C §6.7.4; PHAK ch. 5 (gust load factors: "reduce the aircraft's speed when rough air is encountered") | ✅ Described as what crews do, not as instruction |
| Limit loads are the highest loads expected in normal operation; the rules require the structure to support one and one-half times the limit loads without failure (the 1.5 "factor of safety"); parts may bend or twist under such loads | PHAK ch. 5, "Load Factors and Aircraft Design"; 14 CFR 25.303 (1.5 factor) | ✅ |
| The 1.5 ultimate factor of safety became a formal US Army Air Corps design requirement in March 1934 (HIAD Revision G) | NASA NTRS, *The Ultimate Factor of Safety for Aircraft and Spacecraft*, https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20150003482/downloads/20150003482.pdf | ✅ |
| Boeing's 1995 static test loaded the 777 to ultimate load, 150 percent of the highest loads any airplane would meet in service; fatigue testing 1995–1997 with 100 hydraulic actuators applied 120,000 cycles (taxi, maneuvers, gusts, pressurization), three design lifetimes | Boeing, "777 Quality Information", https://www.boeing.com/commercial/777/quality-information | ✅ |
| The AIM scale: light (slight, erratic changes; slight strain against belts; food service continues), moderate (greater intensity, positive control; definite strain; unsecured objects dislodged; walking difficult), severe (large, abrupt changes; momentarily out of control; objects tossed about), extreme (violently tossed, practically impossible to control, may cause structural damage); "chop" is rhythmic bumpiness without altitude change; high-level turbulence above about 15,000 feet in clear air is reported as CAT | FAA, Aeronautical Information Manual, Chapter 7, Section 1 (PIREPs relating to turbulence, turbulence reporting criteria table and note), https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aim_html/chap7_section_1.html | ✅ Paraphrased, not quoted |
| 2009–2018: turbulence-related accidents were more than a third of all Part 121 accidents and the most common type; most involved serious injury with no aircraft damage; about half occurred in descent or approach; 60% below 20,000 feet; flight attendants the most commonly injured; more than three quarters of flight attendant injuries in the aft section; finding that being seated with the seat belt fastened is the most effective way to prevent a turbulence injury | NTSB Safety Research Report SRR-21/01, *Preventing Turbulence-Related Injuries in Air Carrier Operations Conducted Under Title 14 CFR Part 121* (2021), Executive Summary and Findings, https://www.ntsb.gov/safety/safety-studies/Documents/SS2101.pdf | ✅ The seat-belt point is given as the NTSB's finding, not as advice |
| 2021 NTSB findings: pilot reports spread slowly and non-standardly between ATC facilities; airlines share turbulence observations only internally; a turbulence "nowcast" would help; recommendations to the FAA on standardizing PIREP distribution | NTSB SRR-21/01, Findings and Recommendations | ✅ |
| FAA table of Part 121 serious turbulence injuries 2009–2024 (NTSB data): total 207, of which 40 passengers and 166 crew; page last updated March 20, 2026 | FAA, "Turbulence: Staying Safe", https://www.faa.gov/travelers/fly_safe/turbulence | ⚠️ The table is internally inconsistent: the 2022 row shows 4 passengers and 13 crew but a row total of 18, so the column totals (40 + 166 = 206) do not match the stated total of 207. The page therefore says "about 200 serious injuries" and "roughly four in five were crew" (166 of 207 = 80%) rather than exact figures. |
| Serious injury definition: hospitalization for more than 48 hours, a fracture (other than simple fractures of fingers, toes or nose), or similar | FAA "Turbulence: Staying Safe" (quoting the NTSB definition); NTSB SRR-21/01 | ✅ Summarized as "more than 48 hours in hospital, or a fracture, or similar harm" |
| Airborne weather radar is built to show precipitation, so clear air shows nothing | PHAK ch. 13 (airborne weather radar detects precipitation); AC 00-30C §5.2 (CAT met without visual clues) | ✅ Stated qualitatively, no numbers |
| Everything else (turbulence causes: convective currents, obstructions, cumulus, wind shear; jet streams 5–9 miles up, about 110 mph average, 250 mph or more; CAT near the jet stream and tropopause with nothing visible; wing spars and ribs; WP-3D Orion missions of 8–10 hours through the eyewall; cabin pressurization) | App lessons `turbulence-jetstreams-contrails`, `why-flying-is-safe`, `fuselage-structure`, `wings-flaps-slats` and the WP-3D Orion Hangar card (verified in the app's CONTENT_REVIEW.md) | ✅ |
