| Claim | Source | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Flaps are the most common high-lift device; four common types: plain, split, slotted and Fowler; the slotted flap is "the most popular flap on aircraft today"; flap extension produces a pitching moment that is trimmed out | PHAK, PDF p.156–157 | ✅ |
| Fowler flaps slide backward on tracks and add area as well as camber; the first part of their travel adds much lift for little drag, the last part adds drag with little extra lift | PHAK, PDF p.157 | ✅ |
| A slotted flap ducts high-energy air from the lower surface onto the flap's upper surface, delaying separation; large aircraft often have double- and triple-slotted flaps | PHAK, PDF p.157 | ✅ |
| Leading-edge devices: fixed slots, movable slats, leading-edge flaps and cuffs; some slats are unpowered, held flush by leading-edge pressure and moving forward as the angle of attack rises | PHAK, PDF p.157–158 | ✅ |
| Spoilers reduce lift; high-speed aircraft use them as speed brakes; they are extended immediately after touchdown to transfer weight to the wheels for braking | PHAK, PDF p.146 | ✅ |
| Spoilers can supplement the ailerons for roll control (differential spoilers) | PHAK, PDF p.147 | ✅ |
| Flaperons combine flaps and ailerons; a mixer combines the pilot's separate inputs | PHAK, PDF p.153 | ✅ |
| Trim systems: a trim tab on the elevator's trailing edge on small aircraft; an adjustable stabilizer is another common trim system | PHAK, PDF p.158 | ✅ |
| Takeoff uses a moderate flap setting (high lift, low drag); landing uses fully deployed flaps and slats (high lift, high drag); flaps and slats move along metal tracks; spoilers after touchdown kill lift and add drag | NASA Glenn, "Flaps and Slats" (app source; these specific statements are not in the app's cards) | ✅ |
