## /learn/airport-codes-explained

| Claim | Source | Status |
|---|---|---|
| IATA codes are issued only at an airline's request, usually the first airline planning to fly there; if one airport serves a city, airport and city share the code; a city with several airports may get its own code | IATA, "IATA Location Codes" fact sheet, https://www.iata.org/en/iata-repository/pressroom/fact-sheets/fact-sheet-iata-location-codes/ | ✅ |
| IATA took over the codes in the 1960s when airlines decided they needed a standardized process | IATA fact sheet | ✅ |
| IATA assigns about 40–50 codes a year through an online process run from its Global Delivery Center; there are over 17,000 possible combinations; the applicant may suggest preferred codes | IATA fact sheet | ✅ |
| The Canadian Y convention "is no longer automatically observed" | IATA fact sheet | ✅ |
| Codes are considered permanent and changed only with strong justification, primarily air safety | IATA fact sheet | ✅ |
| The ICAO indicator for airports in the contiguous United States is the three-letter identifier preceded by K; Alaska uses PA, PF, PO and PP; Hawaii PH; Puerto Rico TJ; the US Virgin Islands TI; listings are in ICAO Document 7910; US requests go to the FAA's National Flight Data Center (NFDC) and other States coordinate with ICAO | FAA, Location Identifiers (FAAO 7350.7), section 1-4, https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/LID/0104.htm | ✅ The HTML page is an older edition of the order; the current JO 7350.9 series carries the same wording |
| Orchard Field was selected as Chicago's new airport in 1945; the site had held a Douglas aircraft assembly plant and had four intersecting concrete runways; the City Council renamed it Chicago O'Hare International Airport in 1949 for Lieutenant Commander Edward H. "Butch" O'Hare, a Medal of Honor recipient from Chicago; it opened to commercial traffic in 1955 with 176,902 passengers in its first year; passenger numbers reached 10 million in 1962, when the city called it the "World's Busiest Airport" | Chicago Department of Aviation, "O'Hare History", https://www.flychicago.com/business/CDA/Pages/OHare.aspx | ✅ flychicago.com is the city's airport authority, not on the brief's allowed list; noted as an official municipal source |
| The letters ORD come from "Orchard" | No official page spells out the derivation. The city's history page gives the Orchard Field name and the 1949 renaming; IATA says codes almost never change | ⚠️ Kept as the generally accepted explanation, worded as the code being "a fossil of the airfield's first name"; the app itself chose not to claim it |
| ORD / KORD for O'Hare | FAA AIP, Illinois aerodromes (app source) | ✅ |
| Everything else (GVA; first-three-letters rule and second choice; 1930s two-letter codes, three letters by the late 1940s, LA to LAX in 1947; Canadian Y and weather stations or radio beacons; DIA to IAD because of DCA; about 11,300 codes; ICAO four letters for flight operations; WAS and rail and ferry stations; A321XLR New York to Rome; DC-3 as a 1930s airliner; 707 era) | App lesson `airport-codes` and its Hangar card, plus `golden-age` and `the-707-jet-set` for the DC-3 and 707 (verified in the app's CONTENT_REVIEW.md) | ✅ |
